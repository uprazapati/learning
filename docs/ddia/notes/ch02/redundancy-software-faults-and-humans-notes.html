Redundancy, Software Faults, and Humans

How hardware redundancy works and why it isn't enough, why distributed systems tolerate whole machines and zones instead, why software faults are worse than hardware faults (they hit every node at once), why "human error" is a symptom rather than a cause, and why reliability matters even for ordinary apps. 01 Hardware redundancy, and its limits

The first response to unreliable hardware is usually to add redundancy to the individual hardware components, to reduce the failure rate of the system:

WhereRedundancy
DisksRAID: spreading data across multiple disks in the same machine, so a failed disk doesn't cause data loss
ServersDual power supplies and hot-swappable CPUs
DatacentersBatteries and diesel generators for backup power

Such redundancy can often keep a machine running uninterrupted for years.

Redundancy works best when faults are independent — that is, when one fault happening doesn't change the likelihood of another. But experience has shown significant correlations between component failures, and a whole server rack or an entire datacenter still becomes unavailable more often than we would like.
Why independence matters (my arithmetic) — if two components each fail on a given day with probability 1 in 10,000, independence means both fail on the same day with probability 1 in 100 million (1/10,000 × 1/10,000). Correlation (beyond the book: e.g. the same manufacturing batch, power feed, or rack) makes the second failure much more likely once the first has happened, so the real odds are far worse than that multiplication says.

02 Tolerating whole machines, racks, and zones

Hardware redundancy increases the uptime of a single machine. A distributed system has other advantages, such as being able to tolerate the complete outage of one datacenter. That's why cloud systems focus less on the reliability of individual machines and instead aim to make services highly available by tolerating faulty nodes at the software level.

Availability zones — how cloud providers identify which resources are physically co-located. Resources in the same place are more likely to fail at the same time than geographically separated resources.

My drawing of the idea (beyond the book's figures). The fault-tolerance techniques in the book are designed to survive the loss of an entire machine, rack, or availability zone. They generally work by letting a machine in one datacenter take over when a machine in another datacenter fails or becomes unreachable. The book covers them in Chapters 6 and 10, and in other places.

ApproachWhere the redundancy livesWhat it survives
Hardware redundancyInside one machine or datacenter (RAID, dual power supplies, generators)A failed component, while the machine keeps running
Software fault tolerance (the book's focus)Across machines, racks, and availability zonesLosing a whole machine, rack, or availability zone

Tolerating the loss of entire machines also has operational advantages. A single-server system needs planned downtime whenever you have to reboot the machine, for example to apply operating system security patches.

Rolling upgrade — a multi-node fault-tolerant system can be patched by restarting one node at a time, without affecting the service for users. The book discusses it further in Chapter 5.

My illustration (beyond the book). Four nodes get the same patch one at a time, so three are always serving and users never see an outage. Patching a single server means the service is down while it reboots.

03 Software faults are correlated

Hardware failures can be weakly correlated, but they're still mostly independent: if one disk fails, the other disks in the same machine will likely be fine, at least for a while. Software faults are different.

Why software faults are worse — they are often very highly correlated, because it's common for many nodes to run the same software and so have the same bugs. Such faults are harder to anticipate, and they tend to cause many more system failures than uncorrelated hardware faults.

My illustration (beyond the book). A hardware fault takes out one node. A software bug takes out every node running that code, all at the same moment, which is exactly the case redundancy can't help with.

The book's examples of software faults:

Kind of faultThe book's examples
A bug that makes every node fail at the same time, in particular circumstancesOn June 30, 2012, a leap second made many Java applications hang simultaneously, because of a bug in the Linux kernel. It brought down several internet services.
Because of a firmware bug, all SSDs of certain models suddenly fail after precisely 32,768 hours of operation (less than four years), and the data on them can't be recovered.
A runaway process that uses up a shared, limited resource: CPU time, memory, disk space, network bandwidth, or threadsA process that uses too much memory while processing a large request may be killed by the operating system. A bug in a client library could cause a much higher request volume than anticipated.
A service the system depends on slows down, becomes unresponsive, or starts returning corrupted responses-
Emergent behavior from an interaction between different systemsBehavior that doesn't occur when each system is tested in isolation
Cascading failuresA problem in one component makes another component overloaded and slow, which in turn brings down another component
The 32,768 hours (my arithmetic) — that's about 3.7 years, and 32,768 = 215, which is the kind of number where a counter overflows. Beyond the book: the well-known case is HPE's 2019 warning about some of its SAS SSDs.
Dormant bugs and broken assumptions — the bugs behind these faults often lie dormant for a long time, until an unusual set of circumstances triggers them. That reveals that the software was making some assumption about its environment which is usually true, but eventually stops being true for some reason.

There's no quick solution to systematic faults in software. Lots of small things can help:

  • carefully thinking about assumptions and interactions in the system
  • thorough testing
  • process isolation
  • allowing processes to crash and restart
  • avoiding feedback loops such as retry storms (the book's "When an Overloaded System Won't Recover", in my Describing Performance note, section 03)
  • measuring, monitoring, and analyzing system behavior in production

04 Humans and reliability

Humans design and build software systems, and the operators who keep them running are human too. Unlike machines, humans don't just follow rules: one of their strengths is being creative and adaptive in getting their jobs done. But that also makes them unpredictable, and sometimes leads to mistakes that cause failures, despite the best intentions.

Configuration changes beat hardware — one study of large internet services found that configuration changes by operators were the leading cause of outages, while hardware faults (servers or network) played a role in only 10%–25% of cases.
"Human error" is a symptom, not a cause — it's tempting to call these problems human error and hope to fix them by controlling people more tightly, with stricter procedures and compliance with rules. But blaming people for mistakes is counterproductive. What we call human error is really a symptom of a problem with the sociotechnical system in which people are trying their best to do their jobs. Complex systems also have emergent behavior, where unexpected interactions between components can lead to failures.

05 What actually helps

Technical measures that minimize the impact of human mistakes:

MeasureDetail
Thorough testingBoth handwritten tests and property testing on lots of random inputs
Rollback mechanismsFor quickly reverting configuration changes
Gradual rolloutsOf new code
Monitoring and observabilityDetailed and clear monitoring, and observability tools for diagnosing production issues
Well-designed interfacesThat encourage "the right thing" and discourage "the wrong thing"
Property testing (beyond the book) — instead of writing each test case by hand, you state a rule that must always hold (e.g. decoding an encoded value gives back the original), and a tool such as QuickCheck or Hypothesis tries thousands of random inputs looking for one that breaks it.
The real problem is often priorities — all of these measures cost time and money. In the pragmatic reality of everyday business, organizations often put revenue-generating work ahead of resilience against mistakes: given a choice between more features and more testing, many understandably choose features. So when a preventable mistake inevitably happens, blaming the person who made it doesn't make sense. The problem is the organization's priorities.
Blameless postmortems — a culture organizations are increasingly adopting. After an incident, the people involved are encouraged to share full details of what happened without fear of punishment, so that others in the organization can learn how to prevent similar problems. This can uncover a need to change business priorities, invest in neglected areas, change the incentives for the people involved, or bring another systemic issue to management's attention.

When investigating an incident, be suspicious of simplistic answers. Neither of these is productive:

Blame — "Bob should have been more careful when deploying that change."
Silver bullet — "We must rewrite the backend in Haskell."
Instead — management should take the opportunity to learn the details of how the sociotechnical system works from the point of view of the people who work with it every day, and improve it based on that feedback.

06 How important is reliability? (the book's sidebar)

Reliability isn't just for nuclear power stations and air traffic control. Ordinary applications are expected to work reliably too. Bugs in business applications cost productivity (and create legal risks if figures are reported incorrectly), and outages of e-commerce sites can cost a lot in lost revenue and damaged reputation.

What goes wrongHow bad, in many applications
A temporary outage of a few minutes, or even a few hoursTolerable
Permanent data loss or corruptionCatastrophic
The photo app test — consider a parent who stores all the pictures and videos of their children in your photo application. How would they feel if that database was suddenly corrupted? Would they know how to restore their collection from a backup?
The Post Office Horizon scandal — between 1999 and 2019, hundreds of people managing Post Office branches in Britain were convicted of theft or fraud because the accounting software showed shortfalls in their accounts. Eventually it became clear that many of these shortfalls were due to bugs in the software, and many convictions were overturned. Probably the largest miscarriage of justice in British history, it came from an assumption in English law that computers operate correctly (so evidence produced by computers is reliable) unless there's evidence to the contrary. Software engineers may laugh at the idea that software could ever be bug-free, but that's little solace to the people who were wrongfully imprisoned, declared bankrupt, or even took their own lives after a wrongful conviction caused by an unreliable computer system.
Cutting corners, consciously — in some situations you might choose to sacrifice reliability to reduce development cost (e.g. a prototype for an unproven market). But be very conscious of when you're cutting corners, and keep the potential consequences in mind.
