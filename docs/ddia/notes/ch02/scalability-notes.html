01 What scalability means

A system that works reliably today won't necessarily work reliably in the future. A common reason it degrades is increased load. Maybe it has grown from 10,000 concurrent users to 100,000, or from 1 million to 10 million. Maybe it processes much larger volumes of data than before.

Scalability — the term for a system's ability to cope with increased load.
"You're not Google or Amazon" — people sometimes say "Stop worrying about scale and just use a relational database." Whether that advice applies to you depends on the type of application you're building.
For a new product, keep it simple and flexible — if you're building a new product with only a few users (perhaps at a startup), the main engineering goal is usually to keep the system as simple and flexible as possible, so you can easily change the product as you learn what customers need. Worrying about hypothetical future scale is counterproductive there. In the best case it's wasted effort and premature optimization. In the worst case it locks you into an inflexible design and makes the application harder to evolve.
Scalability is not a one-dimensional label — it's meaningless to say "X is scalable" or "Y doesn't scale." Discussing scalability means asking questions like these:
  • If the system grows in a particular way, what are our options for coping with the growth?
  • How can we add computing resources to handle the additional load?
  • Based on current growth projections, when will we hit the limits of our current architecture?

If your application does become popular and handles a growing load, you'll learn where your performance bottlenecks are and along which dimensions you need to scale. That's the point to start worrying about techniques for scalability.

02 Understanding load

First you need a clear understanding of the current load on the system. Only then can you discuss growth questions such as "What happens if our load doubles?"

How to describe loadThe book's examples
Throughput (most often)Requests per second to a service, gigabytes of new data arriving per day, shopping cart checkouts per hour
The peak of a quantity that variesThe number of users online at the same time, in the social network case study
Other statistics that shape the access patterns, and so the scalability requirementsThe ratio of reads to writes in a database, the hit rate of a cache, the number of data items per user (followers, in the case study)

Maybe the average case is what matters for you, or maybe your bottleneck is dominated by a small number of extreme cases. It all depends on your application. (My link: in the home timelines case study, the extreme cases were the celebrities with millions of followers.)

Once you understand the load, there are two ways to look at what happens when it increases:

Keep the resources fixed — increase the load in a certain way and keep the CPUs, memory, network bandwidth, etc. unchanged. How is the system's performance affected?
Keep the performance fixed — increase the load in a certain way. How much do you need to increase the resources to keep the performance unchanged?

Usually the goal is to keep performance within the SLA (see section 02 of my response-time metrics note) while minimizing the cost of running the system. More computing resources cost more. Some types of hardware are more cost-effective than others, and that can change over time as new hardware becomes available.

03 Linear scalability

Linear scalability — if doubling the resources lets you handle twice the load with the same performance, you have linear scalability. That's considered a good thing.

04 Scaling up vs scaling out

My drawing of the four setups the book describes (beyond the book's figures). Blue boxes are CPUs, purple is RAM, and orange is disk or storage. The orange outline marks what several machines share.

Vertical scaling (scaling up) — the simplest way to give a service more hardware is to move it to a more powerful machine. Individual CPU cores are no longer getting much faster, but you can buy a machine (or rent a cloud instance) with more CPU cores, more RAM, and more disk space.
Shared-memory architecture — on a single machine you get parallelism from multiple processes or threads, and all the threads of a process can access the same RAM. The problem: cost grows faster than linearly. A high-end machine with twice the hardware of a lower-spec one typically costs significantly more than twice as much, and because of bottlenecks it's unlikely to handle twice the load anyway.
Shared-disk architecture — several machines with their own CPUs and RAM store data on an array of disks shared among them, connected over a fast network: network-attached storage (NAS) or a storage area network (SAN). It has traditionally been used for on-premises data warehousing, but contention and the overhead of locking limit how far it scales.
Shared-nothing architecture (horizontal scaling, scaling out) — a distributed system of multiple nodes, each with its own CPUs, RAM, and disks. Any coordination between nodes happens in software, over a conventional network.
Why shared-nothing has become popular —
  • It can potentially scale linearly.
  • It can use whatever hardware has the best price/performance ratio, especially in the cloud.
  • It can more easily add or remove hardware as the load goes up or down.
  • It can be more fault-tolerant, by spreading the system across multiple datacenters and regions.
Its downsides —
  • It needs explicit sharding (Chapter 7).
  • It brings all the complexity of distributed systems (Chapter 9).
Cloud-native: storage separated from compute — some cloud native databases use separate services for storage and for executing transactions, with multiple compute nodes sharing the same storage service. That's a bit like shared-disk, but it avoids the scalability problems of the older systems: instead of looking like a filesystem (NAS) or a block device (SAN), the storage service offers a specialized API designed for what the database needs. (The book's "Separation of storage and compute" in Chapter 1, in my Cloud-Native System Architecture note.)

05 Principles for scalability

No magic scaling sauce — the architecture of systems that operate at large scale is usually highly specific to the application. There's no generic, one-size-fits-all scalable architecture (informally known as magic scaling sauce). For example, these two systems look very different, even though they move the same amount of data:

(My arithmetic check: 100,000 × 1 kB per second = 100 MB/second, and 3 × 2 GB per minute = 6 GB per minute = 100 MB/second.)

Also, an architecture that fits one level of load is unlikely to cope with 10 times that load. On a fast-growing service you'll probably need to rethink the architecture at every order of magnitude of load growth. Since the application's needs will likely change too, it's usually not worth planning scaling needs more than one order of magnitude ahead.

Break the system into smaller, independent parts — a good general principle is to split a system into smaller components that can operate largely independently of one another. It's the idea behind microservices (Chapter 1, in my Microservices and Serverless note), sharding (Chapter 7), stream processing (Chapter 12), and shared-nothing architectures. The challenge is knowing where to draw the line between things that should be together and things that should be apart. (Other books cover design guidelines for microservices. This book covers sharding shared-nothing systems in Chapter 7.)
Don't make it more complicated than necessary —
  • If a single-machine database will do the job, it's probably better than a complicated distributed setup.
  • Autoscaling systems (which add or remove resources automatically as demand changes) are cool, but if your load is fairly predictable, a manually scaled system may have fewer operational surprises.
  • A system with 5 services is simpler than one with 50.
Good architectures usually mix approaches pragmatically.
