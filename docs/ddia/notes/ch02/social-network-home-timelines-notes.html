Social Network Home Timelines

A toy version of X (formerly Twitter), used to show how a perfectly reasonable SQL query stops working at scale — and how moving the work from read time to write time fixes it. From DDIA, Chapter 2 ("Case Study: Social Network Home Timelines").

reading / work done at query time (pull) writing / work done when someone posts (push)

01 The setup — a toy X/Twitter

Users post messages and follow other users. The book says up front this is a huge simplification of how such a service really works, but it's enough to show the problems that show up in large-scale systems. The numbers it assumes:

AssumptionValue
Posts per day500 million, which is ~5,800 posts/second on average
Peak post rateSpikes as high as 150,000 posts/second
Average userFollows 200 people and has 200 followers
The catchVery wide range: most people have only a handful of followers, while a few celebrities (e.g. Barack Obama) have over 100 million

02 Three tables: users, posts, follows

Everything lives in a relational database: one table for users, one for posts, and one for the follow relationships between users.

03 The obvious way: run a query on every read

The main read operation is the home timeline: recent posts by the people a user follows. (Ads, suggested posts from people you don't follow, and so on are ignored for simplicity.) Straight SQL: 

SELECT posts.*, users.* FROM posts
  JOIN follows ON posts.sender_id = follows.followee_id
  JOIN users   ON posts.sender_id = users.id
  WHERE follows.follower_id = current_user
  ORDER BY posts.timestamp DESC
  LIMIT 1000

What the database actually has to do to answer it:

  • Use follows to find everybody current_user is following,
  • look up recent posts by each of those users,
  • sort them all by timestamp and keep the most recent 1,000.

04 Why that falls over: polling

Posts are supposed to be timely, so the requirement is that followers see a new post within five seconds. The simplest way to get that is for each client to re-run the query every five seconds while the user is online. That's polling. With 10 million users online at once:

10M users online ÷ 5 s poll interval = 2M timeline queries/s × 200 followed accounts each = 400M post lookups/s

Each query has to fetch the recent posts of all 200 followed accounts and merge those lists, so 2 million queries a second becomes 400 million lookups a second. Even polling less often, it's a lot. And that's the average case: some users follow tens of thousands of accounts, and for them this query is very expensive and hard to make fast.

05 Flip it: do the work when someone posts

Two changes:

  • Push instead of poll. The server actively pushes new posts to any followers who are currently online.
  • Precompute the answer. A request for a home timeline gets served from a cache instead of running the query.

Concretely: keep a data structure per user holding their home timeline (recent posts by the people they follow). Whenever someone posts, look up all their followers and insert the post into each follower's home timeline, like delivering a letter to a mailbox. When a user logs in, just hand them their precomputed timeline. For new-post notifications, the client simply subscribes to the stream of posts being added to its own timeline.

06 Fan-out

The downside: every post now costs more work, because home timelines are derived data that has to be kept up to date.

Fan-out — when one initial request results in several downstream requests being carried out, the fan-out is the factor by which the number of requests increases. Here, one post by a user with 200 followers means 200 timeline writes, so the fan-out is 200.

Redrawn from Figure 2-2. Each follower only follows some of the posters, so each timeline holds a different subset of the posts. Reading a timeline is now just fetching one precomputed list.

The book's numbers for the push approach:

5,800 posts/s × 200 followers each = ~1.16M timeline writes/s

That's a lot, but it's still a big saving over the 400 million lookups per second the polling query needed (my arithmetic: about 345x less work).

Spikes don't hurt reads — if the post rate spikes because of a special event, the timeline deliveries don't have to happen immediately. They can be enqueued, and posts simply take a bit longer to show up in followers' timelines for a while. Timelines stay fast to load the whole time, because they're served from the cache. (My arithmetic: at the 150,000 posts/s peak, fan-out would be about 30 million writes/s, which is why being able to queue them matters.)

07 Materialization — the general name for this trick

Materialization — precomputing and updating the results of a query. The timeline cache is an example of a materialized view (a concept the book returns to in later chapters).

The materialized view speeds up reads, but in return there's more work to do on writes. Side by side:

Query on read (pull)Materialized timeline (push)
When the work happens Every time someone loads their timeline (every 5 s while polling) Every time someone posts
Load in this example ~400M post lookups/s ~1.16M timeline writes/s
Reading a timeline Join, sort, and merge across everyone you follow Fetch one precomputed list from a cache
Painful extreme A user who follows tens of thousands of accounts A celebrity with millions of followers posting

(My own framing, from the book's numbers: push wins here because reads massively outnumber writes. That's 2 million timeline queries/s against only 5,800 posts/s, so it pays to do the work once per post instead of once per read.)

08 The extreme cases

For most users the write cost is modest, but a social network has to plan for the extremes:

Someone following a huge number of very active accounts — their materialized timeline gets a high rate of writes. But they're not likely reading every post in it anyway, so it's OK to drop some of those writes and show them just a sample of the posts from the accounts they follow.
A celebrity with millions of followers posting — inserting that one post into millions of home timelines is a lot of work, and here dropping writes is not OK. One fix is to handle celebrity posts separately: store them on their own and merge them into the materialized timeline when it's read, instead of fanning them out to millions of timelines up front. In other words, pull for celebrities and push for everyone else. Even with tricks like this, handling celebrities can take a lot of infrastructure.
