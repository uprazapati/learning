Describing Performance

The two numbers people mean by "performance" (response time and throughput), why pushing one hurts the other, what "latency" means in this book, and why averages mislead while percentiles don't. From DDIA, Chapter 2 ("Describing Performance", "Latency and Response Time", "Average, Median, and Percentiles").

response time (what the user feels) throughput (how much work, i.e. capacity and cost)

01 Two kinds of performance metric

Response time — the elapsed time from the moment a user makes a request until they receive the answer. Measured in seconds (or milliseconds, or microseconds).
Throughput — the number of requests (or volume of data) per second the system is processing. For a given amount of hardware there is a maximum throughput it can handle. Measured in "somethings per second."

Mapped back onto the social-network case study:

Throughput metricsResponse time metrics

02 They're linked, because of queueing

At low throughput, a service responds quickly. As load goes up, response time goes up too. The reason is queueing: on a busy system, a newly arrived request usually finds the CPU already handling an earlier request, so it has to wait for that one to finish. As throughput approaches the maximum the hardware can handle, those queueing delays increase sharply.

Redrawn from Figure 2-3. The book only sketches the shape. The marked points (beyond the book) come from the simplest textbook queueing model (M/M/1), where response time = service time / (1 - load). Half busy doubles the wait, 90% busy is 10x, and it heads to infinity as load approaches 100%. Hover a dot for the exact numbers.

03 When an overloaded system won't recover

A system pushed close to its throughput limit can fall into a vicious cycle where it gets less efficient, and therefore even more overloaded:

Retry storm — response times get so long that clients time out and resend their requests, which raises the request rate even further and makes the problem worse.
Metastable failure — even when the load is reduced again, the system stays in its overloaded state until it's rebooted or otherwise reset. It can cause serious outages in production systems.

How to keep retries from overloading a service:

04 Which number matters to whom

Response time is usually what users care about most. Throughput determines the computing resources you need (e.g. how many servers), and therefore the cost of serving a workload. If throughput is likely to grow beyond what the current hardware can handle, capacity has to be expanded.

Scalable — a system is scalable if its maximum throughput can be significantly increased by adding computing resources.

The rest of this section focuses on response times. The book comes back to throughput and scalability later, in "Scalability".

06 Why the same request isn't always the same speed

Response time can vary a lot from one request to the next, even if you keep sending exactly the same request. Lots of things add random delays:

  • a context switch to a background process
  • a lost network packet and a TCP retransmission
  • a garbage collection pause
  • a page fault forcing a read from disk
  • even mechanical vibrations in the server rack

(The book goes deeper on this later, in "Timeouts and Unbounded Delays".)

Queueing delays often account for a large part of that variability. A server can only process a small number of things in parallel (limited by its CPU cores, for example), so it takes only a few slow requests to hold up everything behind them:

Head-of-line blocking — a small number of slow requests holding up the processing of the requests behind them. Even if those later requests have fast service times, the client sees a slow overall response time because of the wait for the earlier request to finish.
So measure response time on the client side — the queueing delay is not part of the service time. A server timing only its own processing will never see the wait, but the user will.

07 Think in distributions, not single numbers

Since response time varies per request, it isn't a single number but a distribution of values you can measure. Most requests are reasonably fast, but occasional outliers take much longer.

Jitter — variation in network delay.

The usual summary number is the average (technically the arithmetic mean: sum all the response times and divide by the number of requests). The mean is useful for estimating throughput limits. But it's not a good metric for your "typical" response time, because it doesn't tell you how many users actually experienced that delay. In the sample above, a few slow outliers pull the mean (183 ms) above the median (163 ms), so most requests were actually faster than "average".

08 Percentiles

Percentiles — sort the response times from fastest to slowest. The median is the halfway point: if the median is 200 ms, half your requests return in under 200 ms and half take longer. That makes it a good metric for how long users typically wait. The median is also called the 50th percentile, or p50.

To see how bad the outliers are, look at higher percentiles. If the 95th percentile is 1.5 seconds, then 95 out of 100 requests take less than 1.5 s, and 5 out of 100 take 1.5 s or more.

Tail latencies — the high response-time percentiles. They matter because they directly affect users' experience of the service.
Amazon's example — Amazon describes response-time requirements for internal services in terms of the 99.9th percentile, even though that affects only 1 in 1,000 requests. The customers with the slowest requests are often the ones with the most data on their accounts because they've made many purchases. In other words, they're the most valuable customers, so it's important the site is fast for them.

But optimizing the 99.99th percentile (the slowest 1 in 10,000) was judged too expensive and not beneficial enough for Amazon's purposes. Very high percentiles are hard to reduce because random events outside your control easily affect them, and the benefits diminish.
