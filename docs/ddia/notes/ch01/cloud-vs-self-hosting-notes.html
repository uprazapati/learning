Cloud vs. Self-Hosting

Every data system eventually asks the same question: run it yourself, or pay someone else to run it? From DDIA, Chapter 1 ("Cloud Versus Self-Hosting").

self-hosted (more control) cloud service (less control)

01 It's really "build vs. buy"

The goal — spend your limited time and money building the things that actually make your product better, and pay someone else for everything that's just routine plumbing.

A simple rule of thumb: if something is a core part of what makes your company special, build and run it yourself. If it's routine and every other company needs the same thing, buy it from someone who specializes in it.

Extreme example from the book: almost no company makes its own CPUs — it's far cheaper to buy them from a chip manufacturer, because making chips isn't what makes those companies valuable.

02 It's a spectrum, not a switch

"Cloud vs. self-hosted" sounds like two options. It's actually three, and there's a smooth trade-off between them: how much of the stack — the software itself, and who keeps it running — do you hand over to someone else?

The middle option is "self-hosting" — you take existing software (open source or commercial) and run it yourself, rather than writing it or handing the whole thing to a vendor.

03 Self-hosting still splits in two

Even "run it yourself" has two flavors, depending on whose hardware it runs on:

  • On premises — your own machines. (The book notes this name sticks even when the server is actually sitting in a rented rack in someone else's datacenter, not literally in your building.)
  • IaaS (Infrastructure as a Service) — a virtual machine rented from a cloud provider — but you still install, configure, and manage the software running on it. This is the part that's easy to confuse with "using the cloud" in the SaaS sense — renting the hardware isn't the same as handing over the operations.

A related question is how you deploy — e.g. using an orchestration tool like Kubernetes to manage many machines at once — but that's a tooling choice layered on top of this decision, not part of it.

04 When self-hosting actually wins

Self-hosting tends to be cheaper when — your team already knows how to run this kind of system, and your load is predictable (you need roughly the same amount of computing power every day, not wild swings).

If both are true, buying your own machines and running the software yourself is often the cheaper option — you're not paying a cloud provider's margin for something you already know how to do well.

05 When the cloud actually wins

The cloud tends to win when — you don't already know how to run the system yourself, or your load swings wildly rather than staying steady.

Hiring and training people just to operate one system gets expensive fast — adopting a cloud service can be quicker than building that expertise in-house (though you still need an operations team either way, just a smaller one).

Variable load is the sharper example. Say you run a big analytical query: for a few minutes you need a lot of machines working in parallel, then the query finishes and those machines sit idle until the next one. If you own that hardware, you paid for peak capacity you use only occasionally. A cloud provider lets you hand those machines back the moment you're done — you only pay while you're actually using them.

06 The real price of the cloud: control

The biggest downside of a cloud service isn't money — it's that you don't control it. What that actually means in practice:

If this happens……here's what you can actually do
It's missing a feature you needAsk the vendor nicely. You can't build it in yourself.
It goes downWait. There's no server for you to go restart.
Something's slow or buggyHard to diagnose — you don't get the internal logs and metrics you'd have on your own machine.
The vendor raises prices or changes the productYou're stuck with it, or forced to migrate — switching is expensive since most cloud services don't share a common, swappable interface.
A political conflict involves the provider's countryYou could be cut off entirely by sanctions.
You have strict privacy/security rules to followYou now have to trust a third party to uphold them for you.

07 In practice: hybrid, not all-or-nothing

Despite all of that, more and more new products get built on cloud services, or mix the two — cloud for some parts, self-hosted for others. But the cloud won't swallow everything:

  • Plenty of systems predate the cloud and were never rebuilt.
  • Some needs are too specialized for any existing cloud service to meet — e.g. high-frequency trading, where you need full physical control over the hardware just to shave microseconds off latency.

Same underlying lesson as the data warehouse/data lake note: there's rarely one right answer for the whole system — the right call depends on what you already know how to run, and how your specific workload actually behaves.
